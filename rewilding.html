Restoration & rewilding

Where restoration meets rewilding in the Northeast

How restoration projects can become building blocks for large-scale rewilding in the Mid-Atlantic and New England.

Restoration and rewilding are often described as opposites. Restoration is hands-on: we collect seed, propagate plants, remove invasives and manage a site toward a target community. Rewilding is usually framed as stepping back, reconnecting large areas and letting natural processes such as grazing, predation, fire and flooding do the work.

In the Northeast, I think the two depend on each other. Centuries of clearing, fire suppression and development have left landscapes that are missing key pieces, and in many places the plant layer itself is incomplete. A meadow without violets cannot support a Regal Fritillary, however large or well connected it is. Natural processes can only sustain diversity that is actually present, so active restoration of the plant layer often has to come first.

Restoration, in turn, gains its long-term meaning from rewilding. Individual restored prairies and meadows are small and isolated. Linked through conserved land, trail and stream corridors, and large public holdings, they become stepping stones for species that need room to move, and the starting points for larger, more self-sustaining landscapes.

Supporting large rewilding in the Mid-Atlantic and New England means working at both scales at once: building the plant and seed supply that restoration needs, including species missing from commercial catalogs, while working with land trusts, municipalities and public landowners to connect and expand the places where those plants can grow.
